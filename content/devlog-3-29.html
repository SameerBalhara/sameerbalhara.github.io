Opening Remarks

Hi everyone, it's been quite a while since the last devlog for First Settlers. While the weekly posts clearly did not keep up, development has continued, and there is a lot to catch up on. The last post ended with a relatively bare landscape, the first custom terrain shader, and grass which brought the scene down to 2.5 FPS. We now have a much fuller world, with different ground vegetation, instanced trees, improved terrain, a modular building system, and the ability to excavate into the landscape itself.

This entry covers several months of work rather than one week's changes. The latest screenshot above gives an idea of how these systems look together: a constructed shelter, a populated landscape, water, distant terrain, and an excavated space below the surface. Some of these systems are considerably further along than others, so we will also get into what is still experimental. If you are interested in the specifics along with accompanying graphics, keep reading!

Terrain Shape, Erosion, and Streaming Continuity

In the last devlog, much of the terrain work was concerned with removing obvious artifacts. Since then, the underlying height generation has received a more substantial overhaul. The current system composes the base landscape first, then applies an erosion-shaped noise pass in world coordinates. This gives us controls over the scale, amplitude, direction, and character of the detail across the world, rather than treating mountains as the only places which need interesting surface shapes. This is procedural height shaping, not a simulation of water physically eroding the ground over time.

Mountain regions have become broader and more sparsely distributed, while lower terrain can produce larger rolling hills. An earlier attempt at creating benches introduced repeated horizontal bands, so that approach was removed. River valleys are shaped after the global erosion pass, allowing broad, flatter valley floors without losing all the surrounding terrain detail. The same height pipeline is used by detailed terrain, distant terrain, collision, and tree placement; otherwise a tree could be seated on a different version of the ground than the one the player sees.

We also addressed holes and overlapping surfaces during terrain handoffs. Previously, an outgoing coarse tile could disappear before the incoming detailed meshes were actually attached. The replacement now has to be ready before the old coverage is released. This is one of those changes which is easy to overlook in a still picture, but quite important when moving through an endlessly generated world.

From Individual Grass Objects to Resident GPU Rendering

The grass in our previous post was deliberately crude: individual prefabs in a regular grid, with very little optimization. That was useful for getting the pipeline started, but it was not a sensible way to cover an entire landscape. Grass now uses a resident GPU rendering path. Candidate placement and the ecological rules are still generated on the CPU, while the GPU selects the visible instances and submits them through indirect drawing.

Resident here means the instance data stays in GPU buffers instead of being rebuilt and uploaded in full every frame. Near and distant representations come from the same deterministic candidate distribution, with complementary transitions so that a clump does not randomly change identity when its representation changes. Distance-based density and frustum culling further reduce what needs to be drawn. The production path can submit the near and far grass in at most two draws per rendered chunk, rather than dividing every chunk into many small instance batches.

Distant trees use a related approach, with stable placements, visibility selection, thinning, and transitions between representations. Nearby standing trees also remain instanced; approaching one activates a separate collision/query proxy rather than turning its visual into an ordinary GameObject. CPU fallback paths remain available. This does not make vegetation free: overlapping grass blades still have a shading and overdraw cost, and retained buffers consume memory. The important improvement is that the rendering architecture is now appropriate for the number of plants we want to display.

Forest Floors, Ground Cover, and Small Details

Vegetation has also become more dependent on where it is growing. Forest interiors should not simply look like grasslands with trees placed on top. The forest floor now blends litter, soil, and moss, with a continuous irregular field controlling grass density. Openings can carry more grass and clover, while moss excludes grass and still allows some fallen leaves to accumulate. Moisture, slope, and the surrounding habitat influence placement.

We added a separate simple forest grass tuft, scattered leaf groups, and ferns with three levels of detail. Leaves vary in occupancy, arrangement, heading, and scale so that the same small stamp is less obvious across the ground. Leaves and ferns now have resident GPU paths as well, and distant leaf density can decrease without reseeding the nearby placements. Clover has its own render range rather than being unnecessarily restricted to the grass range.

Flowers, dandelions, lily pads, cattails, and meadow insects add further variation. Bees and butterflies are restricted to suitable grassland rather than appearing indiscriminately in forests. These details are small individually, but together they help distinguish one part of the landscape from another. The lakeside image above shows an earlier state of this work; the latest image at the top includes the more developed environment and building systems.

Water, Sky, and Distant Atmosphere

The environment now has time-of-day skybox selection for day, twilight, and night, coordinated with the existing clock and lighting. These are material changes at the relevant times, rather than a continuous crossfade between sky textures. A separate distant fog layer gives the low landscape a sense of depth while allowing taller mountain peaks to stay visible above it.

The fog samples through a bounded height region, with adjustable resolution and step count. Water needs particular care because the depth behind a transparent lake can be the lake bed rather than the visible surface. The effect therefore uses coverage from the actual water meshes when determining where to apply fog. Planar water reflections also skip updates when no water is visible and refresh when it comes back into view. These are useful ways to avoid unnecessary work, though the total cost still depends on the scene and chosen quality settings.

Modular Building and Authored Assets

We can now start placing a settlement into the world. The building prototype began with simple foundation, wall, and floor pieces, and has grown into a catalog of authored wood and stone components. The current wood kit follows four-meter wall spans and three-meter storeys, with a quarter-meter placement grid and eight possible horizontal headings. Having a consistent standard is quite important: a wall, floor, stair, and roof can each look fine in isolation yet fail to meet properly when combined.

The kit includes split-plank and wattle walls, posts, framed floors, openings, stairwell floors, stairs, handrails, and thatched roof pieces. Stone walls, piers, floors, caps, boundary walls, and a gate opening provide another set of options. Detailed source models are retained for authoring and baking, while simpler runtime geometry carries baked color, ambient occlusion, and normal detail. As one concrete example, the joined thatch roof was reduced from 922 triangles to 62 while retaining its intended shape and construction.

Placement uses the grid of the structure being aimed at, with contextual snapping, manual rotation, and player-relative nudges. Openings and stairways also need collision which agrees with the usable space, rather than a solid box across every visual gap. Structural support is tracked as connections back to grounded anchors. Disconnected pieces have a short reconnection grace period before removal; this is a connectivity model, not yet a simulation of material strength or weight.

The first six furniture pieces are also installed: a stool, bench, table, low work table, sleeping platform, and wall shelf. Their supports are checked separately without making them carry the building's structural loads. The shelter in the latest image shows how far the scene has come from the terrain-only stage, but building records still last for the current session rather than being saved to disk.

Player Interaction, Inventory, and the Hotbar

World interaction needs a stable identity behind the visible object. A rendered tree, its nearby trunk collider, and a canopy query shape should all resolve to the same generated tree record. We have been separating physical collision, identification, and rendering so these systems can operate at different distances without losing that connection. This also provides a foundation for later resource interactions, although identifying a breakable object does not mean its complete harvesting behavior is implemented.

The inventory and hotbar now have their initial UI, including backpack, equipment, offhand, and crafting areas. Number keys select the hotbar cell, and inventory and gameplay selection stay synchronized. Their scales can be adjusted independently, with the layout responding to the available screen space. However, the slots are currently empty: item transfers, recipes, item ownership, and inventory saves are not implemented yet. Getting a visible interface in place and getting a functioning survival inventory are two separate stages.

Excavation and Building Below the Surface

Ordinary heightmaps can only provide one ground height at each horizontal coordinate. That is sufficient for hills and valleys, but not for a tunnel with a floor and an intact ceiling. The excavation system therefore adds a sparse three-dimensional density layer, sampled at 0.3-meter spacing, over the procedural baseline. Sphere and box edits remove material, and the affected surface is reconstructed into render meshes and collision.

The difficult part is making that edited region coexist with the rest of the world. Mesh boundaries have to join the existing terrain, collision has to be current before movement or placement uses it, and delayed worker results must not overwrite a newer edit. Excavation now stages density, rendering, and collision changes through the shared streaming machinery. Cut surfaces receive stone materials, ceilings cast local shadows, and foliage is removed where its original surface support has actually disappeared. Digging a sealed cave should not erase the grass on the ground above it.

Buildings can also intersect edited terrain, including underground stairs and buried endpoints, while initial footing and structural connection checks remain. Existing grounded anchors survive excavation and streaming; removing an actual building support still follows the normal support rules. We implemented a disk-saving stage during development, but the current sculpting mode deliberately keeps edits for the world session only. Restarting returns to the procedural baseline. Larger edited scenes and live movement performance still need further work, so this remains a development system rather than a finished terrain-editing feature.

Cave Lighting and Local Torches

Once you can dig underground, ordinary outdoor lighting becomes much more noticeable in the wrong places. We added a sparse sky-access field, shared by terrain and building materials, to reduce ambient lighting inside covered spaces while retaining a response near entrances. Direct sun and moon occlusion were corrected separately, since dimming ambient light alone does not stop sunlight from reaching a fully covered floor.

There are now mounted torches in the building catalog and a development carried torch. Their flame animation and mild flicker are paired with bounded local illumination, with a 12-meter cutoff and a current limit of 64 illuminating sources. Stationary shadows can be reused, while moving lights and blockers receive the updates they need. We also implemented and compared a surface-illumination cache, but it remains optional because it did not consistently outperform the spatial direct-lighting path.

This is direct lighting rather than bounced global illumination, and its memory cost is significant: the current full-capacity shadow textures occupy roughly 102 MiB before the optional surface cache. Final quality settings will need more representative hardware testing. Fuel, recipes, and multiplayer ownership are still separate future work.

Keeping the World Within a Frame Budget

All of these additions have to share the same frame time. Terrain publication, content work, foliage scheduling, and grass completion now participate in a shared two-millisecond main-thread allowance. Collision is prioritized ahead of visual LOD changes, queues take turns receiving service, and obsolete results are rejected before allocating Unity resources. Height sampling also shares a cap of six simultaneous tasks, while terrain texture publication defaults to one complete control-map set per frame.

These are soft limits. A single Unity operation can exceed its slice, and rendering and several other systems remain outside the measured pool. Tighter budgets also mean some terrain or foliage arrives later. We have added profiler breakdowns, clearer ownership, and bounded scratch-buffer reuse to make these tradeoffs easier to investigate. Passing isolated checks or looking at the FPS counter in one screenshot does not establish a stable whole-game performance improvement.

Future Goals/Needed Changes

As with the previous devlog, it is difficult to promise exactly what will happen in a particular week. The immediate priorities are making the existing systems work reliably together, checking streaming and excavation during actual movement, and finding suitable performance and memory settings beyond the development machine. Save behavior for terrain, buildings, and eventual inventory contents still needs a coherent design before we can treat those systems as persistent world state.

There is also plenty left on the gameplay side: populated inventory slots, usable resources and recipes, further interaction with the environment, and multiplayer integration. A screenshot can communicate the visual progress, but it cannot show all those missing pieces. We are still interested in building a flexible pipeline and learning what these systems require before calling the result complete.
